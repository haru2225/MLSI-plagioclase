# Training data generation for plagioclase and its high-pressure products

Draft methods section (English) for the MLSI classifier trained on plagioclase, its polymorphs and high-pressure
breakdown products. Numbers refer to the data set in `scripts/md_training_data/` produced by
`scripts/lammps_training_data/run_all.sh`. Items marked **[check]** must be confirmed before submission.

---

## 1.1 Training data generation for plagioclase structures

Training data were generated by classical molecular dynamics (MD) with LAMMPS [1]. All systems, including pure
SiO2, were described with the SHIK potential [2,3], a Buckingham-type pair potential with an additional r⁻²⁴
repulsion and partial charges (Si 1.7755 e, Al 1.6334 e, Na 0.6018 e, Ca 1.3967 e; the O charge follows from charge
neutrality of each composition). The short-range terms were smoothly truncated at 8 Å and the Coulomb interaction
was evaluated with the damped shifted-force Wolf summation [4,5] with a cutoff of 10 Å. We used one potential for
all phases so that the classifier cannot distinguish phases by differences between force fields. The BKS potential
[6] was not used, because it does not stabilise β-cristobalite and tridymite [7]. We used a time step of 1 fs, a
Nosé–Hoover thermostat damping time of 100 fs and a barostat damping time of 1000 fs.

**Crystalline phases.** As input structures we used supercells of 23 crystal structures, constructed from
crystallographic data in the Crystallography Open Database [8] such that every perpendicular cell width is at
least 24 Å (1200–2880 atoms). The set contains the plagioclase end members and an intermediate composition, their
high-temperature polymorphs, the SiO2 polymorphs, and the phases reported in static high-pressure experiments and
in shocked meteorites as breakdown products of plagioclase:

* Plagioclase: low albite [9], anorthite (P1̄) [10] and labradorite An60 (I1̄) [11], with the high-temperature
  forms monalbite (C2/m) [12] and I1̄ anorthite [13].
* Breakdown products of albite: jadeite [14] (with quartz below ~3 GPa and with stishovite up to ~23 GPa),
  NaAlSi3O8 hollandite (lingunite) [15–17] and CaFe2O4-type (CF) NaAlSiO4 [18], which together with stishovite is
  stable above ~24 GPa [15,19].
* Breakdown products of anorthite: grossular [20], kyanite [21] and corundum [22] (above ~14 GPa), the CAS phase
  CaAl4Si2O11 [23–25] (above ~18 GPa) and CaSiO3 perovskite [26] (above ~20 GPa) [24,27]. The Al2SiO5 polymorphs
  andalusite [21] and sillimanite [28] were added together with kyanite.
* SiO2: α-quartz [29], coesite [30], α-cristobalite [31], monoclinic tridymite [32], HP-tridymite [33],
  stishovite [34] and seifertite [35].

Disordered structures were converted into ordered atomistic models. Mixed Al/Si sites were filled at random in the
refined ratio (CF-type NaAlSiO4 1:1, CAS phase M1 2:1, lingunite 1:3). In monalbite, Al and Si were placed at random
in the ratio 1:3 and then swapped until no O bridged two Al atoms (Loewenstein's rule). In labradorite, Al was
placed only on the T sites that carry Al in ordered anorthite, and Ca/Na at random on the merged M sites. Of each
pair of half-occupied split sites (Al in the CAS phase, Ca in I1̄ anorthite) one position was kept at random.
Lingunite was modelled from the KAlSi3O8 hollandite structure [36] with K replaced by Na, and CaSiO3 perovskite by
the ideal cubic perovskite positions at the measured lattice parameter [26].

Each structure was first relaxed together with its cell at the target pressure. It was then equilibrated for 10 ps
under NPT conditions with all cell parameters, including the angles, allowed to change. During a further 1 ps,
500 snapshots were dumped every 2 fs. The standard conditions were 300 and 500 K at 0 and 1 GPa. Phases that are
stable only at high temperature or high pressure were simulated in their stability field instead:

* quartz: α at 100 and 300 K and β at 700 and 1000 K;
* cristobalite: 500, 700, 850 and 1000 K at 0 GPa;
* HP-tridymite: 700, 850 and 1000 K at 0 GPa;
* monalbite: 800 and 1100 K;
* I1̄ anorthite: 500 and 800 K;
* CF-type NaAlSiO4: 25 and 30 GPa.

The α/β transition of quartz was located from the time-averaged Si–O–Si angle, which increases from 148° at
100 K and saturates at about 157° above 600 K. With SHIK, α-cristobalite collapses at 10–400 K and transforms into
the cubic β form above ~500 K, as reported for heating α-cristobalite in MD [37]. The cristobalite data therefore
contain only the β form. Cristobalite and HP-tridymite collapse into denser structures at 1 GPa, so they were
simulated at 0 GPa only. CF-type NaAlSiO4 lost its octahedral coordination at 0–1 GPa (mean Si coordination
number 5.5) and was therefore simulated at 25 and 30 GPa.

We checked every trajectory against its starting structure (`scripts/lammps_training_data/tools/check_crystals.py`).
We compared the density, the Si and Al coordination numbers (cutoff 2.3 Å) and the 90th percentile of the
displacement of the time-averaged atomic positions from the starting sites, with cell deformation removed.
Si stayed four-fold (SiO2 polymorphs with tetrahedra, feldspars) or six-fold (stishovite, seifertite, lingunite, CaSiO3
perovskite, CAS phase, CF-type NaAlSiO4) in all trajectories, and the site displacements are 0.1–0.4 Å for all
phases except tridymite and the two quartz/cristobalite high-temperature forms (0.6–1.3 Å, caused by the rotation of rigid
tetrahedra). Two exceptions were handled as follows. The two 1 GPa trajectories of monoclinic tridymite
(300 and 500 K) increase the density by 7–8 % and change the radial distribution function, which indicates a
collapse to a denser structure, and were excluded. In SHIK, half of the Al in andalusite relaxes from six- to
five-fold coordination (mean Al coordination 5.0 instead of 5.5) without a change of the framework (site displacement
0.3 Å); this is a feature of the model and is kept.

Snapshots of different polymorphs of one structure family share one label: α- and β-quartz are labelled quartz,
monoclinic and HP-tridymite tridymite, and low albite, monalbite, anorthite, I1̄ anorthite and labradorite
plagioclase. This gives 17 crystalline classes and 89 trajectories (44,500 snapshots).

**Melt and amorphous phases.** Melts and glasses were generated for four compositions: SiO2, albite (NaAlSi3O8),
anorthite (CaAl2Si2O8) and labradorite (An60), each with 1872 atoms. Random configurations at 2.3 g cm⁻³ were
melted at 5000 K and equilibrated at 0 and 60 GPa. 500 snapshots each were then dumped at 5000 K and, after
cooling, at 4000 K (16 trajectories). The 4000 K melts were quenched to 300 K at 0 and 60 GPa with quench rates
of 10¹² and 10¹³ K/s, which gives 16 glass models. Following the protocol of Ref. [38], each model was compressed
(0 GPa models, 0→60 GPa) or decompressed (60 GPa models, 60→0 GPa) at a rate of 0.1 GPa/ps at constant
temperature (T = 300, 500, 800 and 1000 K), and 500 snapshots were dumped during each run (64 trajectories). To
include fracture surfaces and under-coordinated environments, the 0 GPa models were also stretched uniaxially at
a strain rate of 10¹⁰ s⁻¹ up to 60 % strain at 300 and 800 K, with zero lateral stress, and 500 snapshots were
dumped up to fracture (16 trajectories). Melt and glass snapshots form the classes melt and amorphous.

**Classification and unknown structures.** The classifier is trained on these 19 classes. In the application,
atoms whose largest softmax score is below 0.5 are assigned to the class other. This class has no training data
and collects local environments that do not resemble any of the training phases, for example high-pressure
phases that are not in the training set (tissintite, Ca-hollandite (stöfflerite) or the CaCl2-type SiO2 phase).
We test this assignment by leaving one crystalline class out of training and checking that its atoms are
classified as other **[check: report the leave-one-class-out result]**.
